The Architecture of Shade

For most of history, cities in hot climates were built to make shadows. Then air-conditioning taught us to forget how. Leila Haddad walks the line between sun and shade — and asks who gets to stand on the cool side of it.

By Leila Haddad

Reading time
16 minutes
Drawings
Ines Achterberg
In print
Issue 14, pp. 8–27

At two o’clock on a July afternoon, the great square in front of the covered market in Ilmara is empty. Not quiet — empty. Its pale limestone paving, laid in the 1970s when the city decided it needed a plaza in the European manner, throws back so much heat that the air above it trembles. On the far side, under the market’s arcade, perhaps two hundred people are doing what people in hot cities have always done in the middle of the day: waiting it out. They sit on the stone ledge that runs between the columns, lean against the piers, sell cold water and phone credit, doze, argue about football. The square is the city’s showpiece. The arcade is where its life is.

I have spent much of the past three years thinking about the line that separates those two places: the hard, moving edge where sunlight becomes shadow. It runs through every city I visited for this essay, and through a great many I did not. It may be the most important line in the design of a hot city, and it almost never appears on a plan.

A material without mass

Shade is a strange thing to design. It has no weight, no cost per square metre and no line in a schedule of finishes. It is rarely photographed for award submissions — at least not at noon, when it matters. It moves: an arcade that lies deep in shadow at ten in the morning can be a strip of glare by four. Shade is less an object than an arrangement between a surface, the sun and the hour, and its success is measured not in the building but in the body: a lower skin temperature, a slower pulse, a decision to walk rather than drive.

And yet for most of urban history, shade was among the first things a city was built to provide. The lanes of old desert towns are not narrow by accident; a street much narrower than its walls are tall stays in shadow for most of the day.Note 1 Courtyards gather the cool of the night and hold it well into the afternoon. Arcades, loggias, colonnades, porticoes — the sheer number of words we have for them says something about how universal the idea is — wrap the public edge of a building in a band of shade that belongs to everyone who passes. The lattice screen, the awning, the pergola heavy with vine: none of these is decoration. Together they make up a climate technology refined over centuries, and that technology was, crucially, civic. Shade was something a city owed to the people walking through it.1 Architects describe the proportion as H/W: the height of the street wall over the width of the street. In a north–south street at about 2 : 1, direct sun reaches the paving only in a short window either side of noon. See Fig. 1.

Shade was something a city owed to the people walking through it. Somewhere in the twentieth century, we stopped paying.

Somewhere in the twentieth century, we stopped paying. Air-conditioning moved comfort indoors and made it private: something you buy by the hour, behind a sealed facade. Streets were widened for cars, and the buildings along them retreated behind setbacks and car parks. The arcade, expensive to build and awkward to let, quietly disappeared from development codes. Mature trees were felled for sightlines, signage and buried cables, and replaced, if at all, by saplings that would need twenty years to cast a useful shadow.

The shade gap

The result, in many fast-growing hot cities, is a public realm that is not merely uncomfortable but, for several months of the year, genuinely dangerous — and dangerous above all for the people who cannot retreat into a cooled car or office: the bus rider, the street vendor, the delivery cyclist, the child walking home from school. Map the surface temperature of almost any such city from above and it will trace the outline of its tree canopy with depressing precision. Map the canopy, and you have traced its incomes. The shade map of a city is very often its wealth map, redrawn in a cooler ink.

This is why I have come to think of shade not as an amenity but as infrastructure: as basic, and as political, as drainage or street lighting. Nobody is expected to bring their own streetlight. Why should anyone have to bring their own shadow?

The good news is that the knowledge was never lost, only mislaid. In Sahrane, a small team in the city’s planning office has spent a decade quietly rewriting the building code, so that every new frontage on a main street must now give back a covered walkway at least three metres deep. Nobody there calls it an arcade; the developers call it the compliance strip. But walk along one at two in the afternoon and you will find a stone ledge, people leaning on the piers and, somewhere, an argument about football.
