01 Cover essay

The Architecture of Shade

For most of history, the hottest cities on earth were organised around a resource nobody thought to measure. As the summers lengthen, we will have to learn to design it again — not as an amenity, but as infrastructure.

By Leila Haddad

24 min read · Diagrams by the author · 22 September 2026

At two o’clock on a July afternoon, the old cloth market in Qasr Ambar is eleven degrees cooler than the car park four hundred metres away. Nobody who works there needs a thermometer to tell them so. The spice sellers set out their sacks along the north wall, where the shadow arrives first; the boy who carries tea times his rounds by the narrowing strip of sun on the paving; and at around four, when the light finally slides off the far roofs, the whole street exhales and the shutters go up again. The street is not air-conditioned. It is simply drawn — its width, its height, its turns and the reed mats strung between its upper windows — so that the sun touches the ground for less than an hour a day.

I have spent much of the past three years walking streets like this one, a notebook in one hand and a cheap infrared thermometer in the other, trying to understand what their builders knew. The short answer is that they treated shade as a material. It could be heaped up, channelled, rationed and shared. It was argued over in court, written into deeds and passed down to children. A neighbour who raised his wall and took the afternoon shadow from your courtyard had taken something from you, as surely as if he had moved your boundary stone.

We have forgotten most of this. For a century or so, cheap energy allowed us to treat heat as a problem to be solved indoors, by machines, one sealed room at a time. Streets were widened for traffic and for light; trees were cut back to protect façades and power lines; squares were paved from edge to edge and left bare in the name of openness. The result, in a great many hot cities, is a public realm that is pleasant for eight months of the year and close to uninhabitable for the other four — and those four months are getting longer.

A shadow you could own

The idea that a shadow might be property sounds faintly absurd until you read the old building codes. Jurists around the Mediterranean argued at length over the right to shade, and the precedents they set were remarkably precise. An upper storey could project over the street only so far as a laden camel could still pass beneath it. An awning could be tied to a neighbour’s wall but never nailed into it. A covered passage — the sabat, which bridges a lane and turns it into a cool tunnel — needed the consent of everyone whose door opened beneath it, because everyone beneath it would share both its relief and its gloom.Note 1

1 Clearances were rarely given in fixed units. They were measured by what had to pass beneath: a rider, a loaded animal, a funeral bier.

What strikes me about these rules is less their ingenuity than their assumption. They take for granted that shade is collective: it is made by private buildings, but it is consumed in public, and the city therefore has a stake in how it is made. A merchant who built tall along a narrow lane was obliged, in effect, to cool it.

Shade is made by private buildings, but it is consumed in public.

Contemporary planning has almost no vocabulary for this. We measure sunlight obsessively — the hours of sun a new tower will steal from a park, the patch of sky visible from a ground-floor window — because our codes were written in cold, damp cities where sunlight was scarce and therefore precious. In hot cities the arithmetic is reversed, but the instruments have never been recalibrated. A planning officer in a desert city can tell you exactly how much sun a new building will block. She will rarely be asked how much shade it will give.

There are signs that this is changing. A handful of municipalities now publish shade maps alongside their flood maps, and a few have begun to treat street trees as infrastructure — with asset registers, maintenance budgets and replacement schedules — rather than as decoration. In Valdorra, where summer afternoons now pass forty-four degrees with grim regularity, the council last year revived a sixteenth-century ordinance obliging shops on the main streets to hang canvas awnings from June to September.Note 2 It is, one officer told me, the cheapest cooling programme the city has ever run.

2 Ordinance 14 of 1563, “On the hanging of cloths over the streets”, was repealed in 1911 to make way for tram wires. Its successor, the council admits, is a near-verbatim copy.

But awnings and tree registers are the easy part. The harder question, and the one this issue keeps returning to, is what it would mean to design a city around the shadows it casts — to draw the shade first, and the buildings second.

08 Interview

A tree is a slow building

The landscape architect Ama Ofori-Lund plants streets for the summers of 2070. She talks to Sami Rahal about soil, patience and why the best shade is the kind nobody notices.

Sami Rahal: You often say the problem with street trees isn’t the trees.

Ama Ofori-Lund It’s the ground. We plant a tree that wants to be twelve metres tall in a pit the size of a fridge, pave over it, and then blame it for dying young. Under every good shade tree there’s a hidden structure — soil, water, room for roots to travel beneath the pavement. That’s where the money should go.

Sami Rahal: And above ground?

Ama Ofori-Lund Patience. A tree is a slow building. It takes twenty years to finish, and then it works for a hundred — quietly, and for free.
