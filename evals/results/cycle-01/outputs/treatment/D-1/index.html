Issue 14: Shade

How do cities in hot climates make shade — and who gets to stand in it? This issue walks under arcades, awnings, trees and screens, from the scale of a window to the scale of a city.

8 pieces · 1 h 46 min of reading · Print edition, 164 pages

01 Cover essay

The Architecture of Shade

For most of history the hottest cities were also the most carefully shaded. Then air conditioning made shade feel optional. Leila Haddad walks from the covered lane to the open car park and asks what it would take to design shade as public infrastructure again.

By Leila Haddad · Drawings by Ines Vidal · 28 min read

At two in the afternoon in late June, the city where I grew up divides itself in two. On one side of every street is the sun, white and total, bouncing off limestone until the pavement seems to hum. On the other side is everyone. Bus queues bend to follow the edge of a wall’s shadow. Fruit sellers drag their crates a metre east every hour. Old men move their chairs the way a sundial moves its hand, without ever looking up.

Nobody designs this choreography, but somebody once designed the shadows that make it possible. The narrow lane, the deep arcade, the projecting upper storey, the lattice screen,1 the row of trees planted thirty years before anyone needed them: none of these were ornaments. They were the city’s cooling system, built from geometry and patience, and they ran without a single kilowatt.1 Known across North Africa and the Middle East as the mashrabiya: a projecting window screened with turned-wood lattice that shades, ventilates and veils at once. Fig. 1 shows one on the west side of the street.

Then, over roughly two generations, we stopped building them. Streets were widened for cars and for light. Arcades were glazed in to make shopfronts. Trees came out for drainage, for parking, for sightlines. Cooling moved indoors, behind sealed glass, and the outdoor city was left to the sun — a place to cross quickly, from one conditioned box to the next. Shade, which had been a shared good, became a private one: something you paid for with a car, an office, a membership.

Measuring what we lost

Climate scientists have a blunt way of describing the result. On a summer afternoon an unshaded asphalt car park can reach a surface temperature well above 60 °C, while the ground beneath a mature tree a few metres away may be twenty degrees cooler. The air between them differs far less. But a body does not feel air temperature alone; it feels radiation, the heat thrown at it by the sky and by every hot surface nearby — and radiation is precisely what shade removes.

This is why the thermometer in the weather report says so little about how a street feels. The figure that matters to a body is closer to what researchers call mean radiant temperature,2 and in a sunlit plaza it can exceed the air temperature by twenty or thirty degrees. Step beneath an awning and most of that excess disappears at once. No other intervention in the public realm — not misting fans, not pale paving, not even water — does as much, as quickly, for as little.2 Mean radiant temperature combines the heat a body receives from the sun, the sky and the surfaces around it into a single figure. It is the main reason a shaded bench feels far cooler than the air temperature suggests.

Shade is not the absence of sun. It is a material, and like any material it can be designed, shared or withheld. The old builders understood its properties precisely. They knew that a street twice as tall as it is wide keeps its floor in shadow for most of the day,3 and that the hour around noon, when the sun stands almost overhead, is the one an overhang or a sail has to cover. They knew that a lattice screen shades a room without stopping its air, and that a canvas stretched between rooftops costs almost nothing and can come down in winter, when the sun is welcome again.3 The height-to-width ratio, H : W, is the simplest measure of how much a street shades itself. The street in Fig. 1 is about 1.6 : 1.

None of this knowledge is lost, exactly. It survives in textbooks, and in the old quarters that visitors photograph and residents quietly prefer. What has been lost is the assumption behind it: that the space between buildings is a room the city owes its citizens, and that a room needs a roof.

I spent the past year walking the hottest hours of six cities with a cheap infrared thermometer and a notebook, looking for places where that assumption survives, or has been rebuilt. I found it in a bus station whose concrete canopy was shaped, sixty years ago, to throw its shadow exactly where the queue forms at five o’clock. I found it in a new housing block whose architect fought the planning office for the right to project its upper floors over the pavement, and won. Most often, I found it in improvisation: shop awnings stitched edge to edge across a lane, bedsheets knotted between balconies, plastic chairs that appear every afternoon beneath one particular tree.

Those improvisations are the most honest data a city produces. They show exactly where shade is needed and exactly where design has failed to supply it. This essay asks what would happen if we read them not as clutter to be cleared, but as a brief.

08 Interviews

Plant the Shade First

Landscape architect Marisol Quintero has spent twenty years planting public squares in cities where summer lasts eight months. She talks to Daniel Osei about drawing shadows before trees, the politics of irrigation, and why the best shade takes a decade to arrive.

DODaniel Osei: You say every project starts with a drawing of a shadow.

MQMarisol Quintero: Of the shadow I want at four in the afternoon in August, yes. Where will someone wait for a bus, where will a child play, where will the old men argue? I draw those patches of dark first. Only then do I ask which tree, or which roof, can cast them.

DODaniel Osei: And if the tree takes ten years to grow?

MQMarisol Quintero: Then you build a cheap roof for the first ten years and take it down when the tree is ready. Cities are bad at temporary things. They should get better at them.

Interview by Daniel Osei · 16 min read
