Issue 14: Shade , Autumn 2026

How hot cities make shade — and who gets to stand in it.

Cover essay In print, pages 8 to 27pp. 8–27

The Architecture
of Shade

For most of history, people building in hot places treated shadow as a material — something to be measured, shaped and shared. Then air conditioning made it seem optional. Leila Haddad on the walls, streets and habits that once kept cities cool, and what it would take to build them again.

By Leila Haddad · 16 min read · Drawings by the author

At two o’clock on an August afternoon, a hot city tells the truth about itself. Shutters come down, traffic thins, and the streets resolve into a map drawn in two colours: the places where the sun falls and the places where it does not. Walk through any old quarter at that hour and you will find yourself following the second map without being told to — crossing to the shaded side, slowing beneath an arcade, lingering at the mouth of an alley where the air seems, for no obvious reason, to move.

None of this is accidental. For most of the history of building in hot climates, shade was not an amenity added at the end of a project. It was the project. The width of a street, the height of the walls along it, the angle at which it met the wind, the depth of a window reveal, the reach of a balcony, the species of tree planted in a square: each was a decision about where shadow would fall, at what hour, and on whom.

We have lost the habit of thinking this way. The modern hot city — glazed, air-conditioned, organised around the car — treats the sun as a problem to be solved indoors, by machines. Outside, between the buildings, the problem is simply left where it is. The pavement becomes something to cross as quickly as possible, from one cooled interior to the next. The street, which was once the coolest room in the city, has become the hottest.

“The street, which was once the coolest room in the city, has become the hottest.”

A street is a sundial

The oldest shade device is not a canopy or a tree but a proportion. Divide the height of the buildings that line a street by the width of the space between them and you have what climate engineers call the aspect ratio of the street canyon. Anyone who has walked through an old medina in July understands it with their body. Where the walls are twice as tall as the street is wide, direct sun reaches the ground for only a few hours either side of noon. Where they are four times as tall, it hardly arrives at all.

Fig. 1 shows the threshold. At a height-to-width ratio of 2 : 1, a sun lower than 63° across the street cannot reach its floor; at 4 : 1 the angle rises to 76°, which a summer sun in the subtropics clears for barely an hour at noon.

These proportions were not a matter of taste. They were tuned, over centuries, by people who had no other way to stay cool. A street that runs east to west keeps one side in sun for most of the day, so the main streets of many desert towns bend and kink, breaking long runs of exposure into short ones. Upper storeys lean out over the street on timber brackets, narrowing the strip of sky. In places the street is roofed outright for a few metres — a passage beneath a house, a vault between two walls — and those short, dim tunnels are where neighbours stop to talk.

In parts of North Africa a passage roofed by a room built across the street is called a sabat. Where they survive, they are still where people wait out the afternoon.

It is tempting to file all of this under heritage: beautiful, instructive and impossible to repeat. A lane two metres wide cannot carry a bus or a fire engine. Buildings tall enough to shade it would, under most modern codes, steal daylight from their neighbours. The medina is not coming back, and we should be wary of anyone who sells nostalgia as climate policy.

But the principle beneath the medina is not a style. It is a way of measuring. It asks of any piece of a city: where will the shadow be at four o’clock in July, and who will be standing in it? That question can be put to a six-lane avenue as easily as to an alley. It can be answered with a row of trees, a colonnade, a sail stretched between façades, a deep porch on a school, a bus shelter that faces the right way. What it cannot be answered with is nothing — which is, too often, what we build.

Who stands in the shade

Consider who needs shade most. Not the office worker who moves from car park to lift to desk, nor the shopper driving between air-conditioned malls, but the child walking to school, the man waiting twenty minutes for a late bus, the street vendor, the builder, the woman carrying her shopping home on foot. Heat is unevenly shared in a city, and so is relief from it. A map of tree cover in almost any hot metropolis is also, with depressing accuracy, a map of income.
